SECTION 1.1: IRON & STEEL BASICS

IRON ORE TYPES:

Hematite (Fe2O3):
- Iron content: 70% (highest)
- Color: Red to black
- Most common ore
- Formula: Fe2O3 (iron oxide)
- Locations: Minnesota, Michigan, Brazil, Australia
- Quality: Excellent for steel making
- Reduction: Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2
- Temperature required: 1200°C minimum

Magnetite (Fe3O4):
- Iron content: 72%
- Magnetic properties (easy to identify)
- Color: Black, metallic luster
- Formula: Fe3O4 (mixed iron oxide)
- Locations: Sweden, Russia, Chile
- Reduction: Fe3O4 + 4CO → 3Fe + 4CO2
- Temperature: 1100-1200°C

Limonite (FeO(OH)·nH2O):
- Iron content: 40-50%
- Bog iron (swamp deposits)
- Hydrated iron oxide
- Color: Yellow-brown
- Easy to smelt (low temperature)
- Historic use (colonial America)
- Contains water (must dry first)

Siderite (FeCO3):
- Iron content: 48%
- Iron carbonate
- Less common today
- Roasting required before smelting
- Reaction: FeCO3 → FeO + CO2 (500°C)

IRON SMELTING PROCESS:

Bloomery Furnace (Primitive):

Construction:
- Clay or stone structure
- 3-4 feet tall
- 18 inches diameter
- Bottom air hole (tuyere)
- Top charging hole

Materials:
- Iron ore (crushed to walnut size)
- Charcoal (fuel + reducing agent)
- Flux (limestone) - removes impurities
- Ratio: 2 parts charcoal : 1 part ore

Process:
1. Build fire in bottom
2. Add charcoal layer (6 inches)
3. Add ore + flux layer (3 inches)
4. Repeat layers until full
5. Blow air continuously (bellows)
6. Temperature reaches 1200-1400°C
7. Iron ore reduces to sponge iron (bloom)
8. Slag (impurities) melts, drips to bottom
9. After 6-8 hours, break open furnace
10. Extract bloom (spongy iron mass)
11. Hammer while hot (consolidate, remove slag)

Yield: 50-60% of ore weight becomes iron
Quality: Wrought iron (low carbon, tough, not hardenable)

Blast Furnace (Advanced):

Construction:
- 30+ feet tall
- Brick lined steel shell
- Multiple levels
- Continuous operation
- Much more efficient than bloomery

Charging (top):
- Alternate layers iron ore, coke, limestone
- Ratio: 2 tons ore : 1 ton coke : 0.5 ton limestone

Air Blast (bottom):
- Heated air (800-1000°C) blown in
- Creates intense heat (1600-1800°C)
- Carbon monoxide reduces ore

Reactions:
1. C + O2 → CO2 (combustion)
2. C + CO2 → 2CO (carbon monoxide formation)
3. 3CO + Fe2O3 → 2Fe + 3CO2 (reduction)
4. CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 (limestone decomposition)
5. CaO + SiO2 → CaSiO3 (slag formation)

Output:
- Molten pig iron (tapped from bottom)
- Slag (tapped separately, floats on iron)
- Temperature: 1500°C
- Carbon content: 3-4% (high)
- Brittle, not directly usable

Converting Pig Iron to Steel:

Bessemer Process:
1. Molten pig iron in converter (pear-shaped vessel)
2. Blow air through molten iron
3. Oxygen burns out carbon, silicon, manganese
4. Violent reaction (sparks, flames)
5. Temperature rises to 1650°C
6. When flame color changes (carbon gone), stop
7. Add controlled carbon back (precise amount)
8. Result: Steel with desired carbon content
9. Time: 15-20 minutes per batch

Open Hearth (Slower, more control):
- Large shallow furnace
- Molten pig iron + scrap steel
- Fuel: Gas or oil
- Temperature: 1600-1650°C
- Time: 6-8 hours (vs 20 min Bessemer)
- Better quality control
- More uniform composition

Electric Arc Furnace:
- Modern method (requires electricity)
- Electrodes create arc (3000°C)
- Melts scrap steel
- Very clean steel
- Temperature precise control
- Not practical without grid power

CARBON CONTENT IN IRON/STEEL:

Pure Iron (<0.02% C):
- Very soft
- Not hardenable
- Excellent malleability
- Rare in practice
- Not useful for tools

Wrought Iron (0.02-0.08% C):
- Tough, ductile
- Welds easily
- Doesn't harden by quenching
- Good for decorative work, chains
- Historic use: horseshoes, nails, hinges

Low Carbon Steel (0.08-0.30% C):
- Mild steel
- Machines well
- Welds easily
- Hardens slightly when quenched
- Uses: Structural steel, car bodies, wire

Medium Carbon Steel (0.30-0.60% C):
- Harder than mild steel
- Can be heat treated (hardened)
- Good balance strength/ductility
- Uses: Shafts, gears, railway wheels
- Most common for general forging

High Carbon Steel (0.60-1.00% C):
- Hard when heat treated
- Less ductile
- Takes excellent edge
- Uses: Knives, swords, springs, cutting tools
- Requires care in forging (cracks if cold)

Cast Iron (2.0-4.0% C):
- Very hard, brittle
- Cannot be forged (shatters)
- Excellent castings
- Uses: Engine blocks, cookware, pipe
- Melts at lower temperature than steel (1150-1200°C)

Ultra-High Carbon Steel (>1.0% C):
- Extremely hard
- Very brittle
- Difficult to work
- Special applications only

PHASE DIAGRAMS:

Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram (Critical):

Key Temperatures:
- A1 (727°C / 1341°F): Eutectoid temperature
- A2 (768°C / 1414°F): Curie point (magnetic)
- A3 (910°C / 1670°F): Austenite formation starts

Phases:
- Ferrite (α-iron): Soft, magnetic, BCC crystal
- Austenite (γ-iron): Face-centered cubic, non-magnetic
- Cementite (Fe3C): Iron carbide, very hard, brittle
- Pearlite: Lamellar structure (ferrite + cementite)
- Martensite: Hardened phase (quenched austenite)

Critical Points (0.8% carbon steel):
- Below A1: Ferrite + Pearlite
- A1-A3: Austenite + Ferrite
- Above A3: Austenite (FCC structure)
- Quenched from austenite: Martensite (very hard)

HEAT TREATMENT PRINCIPLES:

Hardening:
1. Heat steel to austenitizing temperature
   - Low carbon: 900-950°C
   - High carbon: 750-850°C
   - Hold 15-30 minutes (soak time)
2. Quench rapidly in water or oil
   - Water: Fast quench, risk of cracking
   - Oil: Slower, less cracking
   - Brine: Faster than water (severe)
3. Result: Martensite formation (very hard, brittle)

Tempering (Reduce brittleness):
1. Reheat hardened steel to lower temperature
2. Temperature determines final properties:
   - 150-200°C: Very hard, brittle (files, cutting tools)
   - 300-400°C: Hard, less brittle (springs, swords)
   - 500-600°C: Tough, moderate hardness (chisels, punches)
   - 650-700°C: Soft, very tough (nearly annealed)
3. Hold 1-2 hours at temperature
4. Air cool
5. Hardness decreases, toughness increases

Annealing (Full softening):
1. Heat to austenite temperature
2. Hold 30-60 minutes
3. Cool VERY slowly (in furnace)
4. Result: Soft, machinable steel
5. Removes internal stresses
6. Used before machining or cold working

Normalizing:
1. Heat to austenite temperature
2. Hold briefly
3. Air cool (faster than annealing)
4. Refines grain structure
5. More uniform properties
6. Used after forging

Case Hardening (Surface hardening):

Pack Carburizing:
1. Low carbon steel part in box
2. Pack with high-carbon material (bone charcoal, leather)
3. Seal box (prevent oxygen)
4. Heat to 900-950°C
5. Hold 4-8 hours (carbon diffuses into surface)
6. Quench
7. Result: Hard surface, tough core

Cyanide Bath (Dangerous!):
1. Molten sodium cyanide bath
2. Part submerged 15-30 minutes
3. Carbon and nitrogen diffuse in
4. Quench
5. Very hard surface
6. ⚠️ EXTREMELY TOXIC - Cyanide gas lethal

Flame/Induction Hardening:
1. Heat surface only (torch or induction)
2. Quench immediately
3. Core remains soft
4. Surface hardens

GRAIN STRUCTURE:

Grain Size Effects:
- Fine grain: Stronger, tougher
- Coarse grain: Weaker, more brittle
- Grain growth: Excessive heating
- Grain refinement: Normalizing

Deformation:
- Cold working: Increases strength, decreases ductility
- Hot working: Above recrystallization temperature
- Work hardening: Strain hardening from cold work
- Annealing: Removes work hardening

ALLOYING ELEMENTS IN STEEL:

Carbon (C):
- Primary hardening element
- Increases hardness, decreases toughness
- Forms carbides

Manganese (Mn):
- Deoxidizer
- Increases hardenability
- Improves grain structure
- Typical: 0.3-0.8%

Silicon (Si):
- Deoxidizer
- Spring steels (higher Si)
- Increases strength
- Typical: 0.1-0.3%

Chromium (Cr):
- Increases hardness
- Corrosion resistance (stainless >12%)
- Forms hard carbides
- Tool steels: 0.5-1.5%
- Stainless: 12-18%

Nickel (Ni):
- Toughness
- Corrosion resistance
- Lowers critical temperatures
- Maraging steel: 18% Ni

Molybdenum (Mo):
- High temperature strength
- Prevents temper brittleness
- Tool steels, high-speed steel
- Typical: 0.2-0.5%

Vanadium (V):
- Grain refinement
- Forms very hard carbides
- High-speed steel
- Small amounts (0.05-0.2%)

Tungsten (W):
- High-speed steel (18% W)
- High temperature hardness
- Tool steels
- Dense, expensive

Cobalt (Co):
- High-speed steel (5-12%)
- High temperature performance
- Expensive

Sulfur (S):
- Machinability (free-machining steel)
- Reduces toughness
- Typ: 0.08-0.13% (free-machining)
- Normally undesired (<0.05%)

Phosphorus (P):
- Undesired (brittleness)
- Keep below 0.04%
- Cold shortness

COMMON STEEL GRADES:

AISI/SAE Numbering:
- 10XX: Plain carbon steel
- 11XX: Free-machining (S)
- 12XX: Free-machining (S+P)
- 41XX: Chromium-Molybdenum

Stainless Steels:
- 304: 18Cr-8Ni (most common)
- 316: 18Cr-10Ni-2Mo (marine)

- 440C: High carbon (knives)

IDENTIFYING STEEL:

Spark Test:
- Grind steel on wheel
- Observe sparks
- Low carbon: Few, long streamers
- Medium carbon: More bursts
- High carbon: Many bursts, bright
- Cast iron: Short red sparks
- Stainless: Few, red-orange sparks
- High-speed steel: Red, forked

File Test:
- Soft: File bites easily
- Hardened: File skates off

Hardness Testing:

Rockwell (RC):
- Diamond indenter
- Load applied
- Depth measured
- RC 60-65: Very hard (files, cutting edges)
- RC 50-55: Hard (knives, tools)
- RC 40-45: Medium (springs)
- RC 20-30: Soft (structural steel)

Brinell (HB):
- Ball indenter
- Larger scale
- HB 100-150: Soft steel
- HB 300-400: Hard steel

SECTION 2.1: FORGE SETUP

FORGE TYPES:

Coal/Coke Forge (Traditional):

Construction:
- Firebrick hearth
- Air source (blower or bellows)
- Tuyere (air pipe)
- Clinker breaker (grate)
- Dimensions: 12"×12" fire pot

Fuel:
- Bituminous coal (best)
- Coke (coal heated without oxygen)
- Charcoal (cleaner, lower temperature)

Operation:
1. Build small coke bed in center
2. Blow air gently
3. Add coal around edges
4. "Banking" - pack coal around fire
5. Maintain clean fire (remove clinker)

Temperature Control:
- Air volume = temperature control
- Deep fire = concentrated heat
- Shallow fire = broader heat
- Max temperature: 1300-1400°C

Propane Forge (Modern):

Construction:
- Refractory lined chamber
- Burner (Venturi or forced air)
- Insulated body (ceramic fiber)
- Door/opening for work

Advantages:
- Clean (no coal dust)
- Precise temperature control
- Easy to maintain
- Fast heating

Burner Types:
- Venturi (atmospheric): Simple, less efficient
- Forced air (blower): More efficient, hotter

Temperature: Up to 1400°C with proper burner

Charcoal Forge:

Simplest Setup:
- Hole in ground
- Pipe for air
- Charcoal fuel
- Primitive but effective

Temperature: 1100-1300°C (limited)

ANVIL:

Anvil Anatomy:
- Face: Flat work surface (hardened)
- Hardy hole: Square hole (hold tools)
- Pritchel hole: Round hole (punching)
- Horn: Tapering cone (curves, rings)
- Step: Softer area (cutting, bending)
- Heel: Back flat area

Anvil Weight:
- Light: 75-100 lbs (portable)
- Medium: 150-200 lbs (most versatile)
- Heavy: 300-500 lbs (production work)
- Rule: Heavier = better (absorbs shock)

Anvil Materials:
- Cast steel: Best quality, expensive
- Forged steel: Very good
- Cast iron with steel face: Adequate
- Railroad rail: Improvised (limited)

Anvil Mounting:
- On wood stump or stand
- Height: Knuckles height when standing
- Secure firmly (chain or lag bolts)
- Don't mount rigidly to concrete (damage)

HAMMERS:

Cross Peen:
- Peen perpendicular to handle
- Drawing out metal (lengthening)
- Most versatile forging hammer
- Weights: 2-4 lbs typical

Straight Peen:
- Peen parallel to handle
- Less common
- Specific shaping tasks

Ball Peen:
- Rounded peen
- Riveting, peening
- Not ideal for forging

Sledge Hammer:
- 6-12 lbs
- Two-hand use
- Heavy forging, striking tools
- Striker uses (with helper)

Rounding Hammer:
- Slightly rounded face
- Finish work
- Less marking on steel

Planishing Hammer:
- Very smooth face
- Final finishing
- Removes hammer marks

Hammer Technique:
- Grip near end of handle (power)
- Wrist snap (control)
- Let hammer do the work
- Rhythm: 60-80 blows per minute
- Strike square to work

TONGS:

Types:
- Flat jaw: Flat stock
- V-bit: Round stock
- Box jaw: Square stock
- Pick-up: Quick grab
- Bolt: Holding bolt heads

Sizing:
- Match tong jaw to workpiece
- Proper fit = safety
- Too loose = dangerous
- Too tight = can't remove

Making Tongs:
- First project for beginning smith
- 3/8" or 1/2" square stock
- Forge jaws, punch rivet hole
- Rivet together
- Adjust jaw fit

TOOLS:

Hardy Tools (fit in hardy hole):
- Hot cut: Cutting hot metal
- Cold cut: Cutting cold metal (dull blade)
- Bending fork: Controlled bends
- Fuller: Grooving, drawing out
- Swage: Shaping round stock

Swage Blocks:
- Cast iron block with various shapes
- Forming channels, grooves, spheres
- Top and bottom swages match

Punches:
- Round, square, drift punches
- Tapered (starting punch)
- Straight (finishing punch)
- Material: Tool steel (hardened)

Chisels:
- Hot chisel: Thick edge (cutting red-hot)
- Cold chisel: Thin edge (cutting cold)
- Set (bottom fuller): Sits on anvil

Files:
- Bastard cut: Rough filing
- Second cut: Medium
- Smooth cut: Finishing
- Draw filing: Long smooth strokes

SAFETY EQUIPMENT:

Essential:
- Safety glasses (MANDATORY)
- Leather apron
- Cotton clothing (no synthetics - melt)
- Leather gloves (handling hot work)
- Ear protection (hammering noise)
- Fire extinguisher (ABC rated)

Shop Setup:
- Ventilation (coal smoke, fumes)
- Water bucket (quenching, emergencies)
- First aid kit
- Clear floor (trip hazards)
- Good lighting

Hazards:
- Flying scale (wear glasses!)
- Burns (hot metal stays hot LONG time)
- Fire (sparks, hot work)
- Noise (hearing damage)
- Inhalation (coal fumes, metal oxides)

FUEL SOURCES:

Bituminous Coal:
- Soft coal
- Higher sulfur
- Produces clinker (fused ash)
- Good forging temperature
- Abundant

Coke:
- Coal baked without oxygen
- Very clean burning
- Fewer impurities
- Higher temperature
- Historic use: blast furnaces

Charcoal:
- Wood carbonized
- Very clean
- Lower temperature
- No clinker
- Historic use, bushcraft

Propane:
- Clean, convenient
- Precise control
- Expensive (fuel cost)
- Requires regulator, hoses
- Modern convenience

Natural Gas:
- Similar to propane
- Cheaper if piped
- Lower BTU (bigger burners needed)

SECTION 2.2: BASIC FORGING OPERATIONS

DRAWING OUT (Making longer, thinner):

Technique:
1. Heat to yellow-orange (1100-1200°C)
2. Strike at angle (upsetting = flat, angling = lengthen)
3. Rotate 90° between blows
4. Work quickly (maintain heat)
5. Reheat as needed

Fuller Use:
- Place fuller on top
- Strike fuller (concentrates force)
- Creates groove
- Stretches metal efficiently

Tapering:
- Draw to point
- Rotate frequently
- Even blows
- Smooth taper

UPSETTING (Making shorter, thicker):

Method:
1. Heat end bright yellow (1200°C)
2. Strike end-on (vertical)
3. Keep straight (doesn't bend)
4. Rapid heavy blows
5. Cools quickly (reheat often)

Localized Upsetting:
- Heat only small section
- Water quench adjacent areas (keep cool)
- Upset heated section
- Used for rivet heads, bolt heads

BENDING:

Right Angle Bend:
1. Mark bend location
2. Heat to yellow
3. Place mark at edge of anvil
4. Strike overhanging end
5. Check with square

Curved Bends:
- Use horn for gradual curves
- Control radius with hammer placement
- Even heating critical

Scrolls:
1. Taper end
2. Bend end into small curl
3. Continue bending, working back
4. Use horn or bending fork
5. Classic decorative element

TWISTING:

Process:
1. Heat section to twist
2. Secure one end (vise or wrench)
3. Twist other end (wrench or pliers)
4. Count turns (symmetry)
5. Quench or air cool

Square Bar Twist:
- Creates "barley twist" pattern
- Requires even heating
- Multiple bars twisted together possible

Round Bar Twist:
- Less dramatic appearance
- Easier to twist (no corners)

PUNCHING:

Punch from Both Sides:
1. Heat to yellow-white (1250°C)
2. Start punch from one side
3. When punch tip bulges other side, flip
4. Punch from second side
5. Removes slug
6. Prevents mushrooming edges

Drift (Enlarge hole):
- Insert tapered drift
- Strike drift
- Stretches hole larger
- Work from both sides

SPLITTING:

Technique:
1. Mark split line
2. Hot cut partway through
3. Insert wedge
4. Spread apart
5. Creates multiple tines (fork, etc.)

FORGE WELDING:

Most Difficult Forging Skill:

Requirements:
- Very high heat (yellow-white, 1300°C)
- Clean surfaces (no oxide)
- Flux (borax) prevents oxidation
- Quick, firm hammer blows
- Experience

Process:
1. Heat both pieces to white heat
2. Apply flux (borax melts, flows)
3. Join pieces quickly
4. Strike firmly, not too hard
5. Overlapping blows
6. Weld spreads outward
7. Reheat and work as needed

Flux:
- Borax (sodium tetraborate)
- Melts at ~740°C
- Protects from oxidation
- Bubbles out, leaving clean metal

Scarfing (Preparation):
- Taper edges to join
- Increases surface area
- Better weld

Fire Welding Layered Steel:
- Damascus/Pattern welded steel
- Stack layers (high/low carbon)
- Forge weld together
- Fold and weld repeatedly
- Creates hundreds of layers

SECTION 2.3: HEAT COLORS & TEMPERATURES

HEAT COLOR CHART (For Steel):

Black Heat: <500°C (930°F)
- No visible glow
- Too cold for forging
- Risk: Cracking if hammered

Blood Red: 550°C (1020°F)
- Faint red glow in darkness
- Still too cold for forging

Dark Cherry: 700°C (1290°F)
- Dark red, visible in daylight
- Minimum forging temperature (light work)
- Risk of cracking (hard steels)

Cherry Red: 800-900°C (1470-1650°F)
- Bright cherry red
- Good forging temperature
- Most tool steels
- Non-magnetic (above Curie point)

Orange: 1000-1100°C (1830-2010°F)
- Bright orange
- Excellent forging temperature
- Mild steel, wrought iron

Yellow: 1200-1300°C (2190-2370°F)
- Bright yellow
- Hot working temperature
- Forge welding temperature (high end)

White: 1300-1400°C (2370-2550°F)
- Yellow-white to white
- Forge welding temperature
- Danger: Burning (sparking = overheated)
- High carbon steel: Can burn

Sparking White: >1400°C
- Sparks flying off
- OVERHEATED
- Steel is burning (too hot!)
- Irreversible damage

TEMPERING COLORS (After Hardening):

Straw (230°C): Light tools, scrapers, files
Purple (270°C): Axes, knives (hard)
Blue (300°C): Swords, springs
Gray-Blue (330°C): Cold chisels, punches

Method:
1. Harden tool
2. Polish surface (bright metal)
3. Heat slowly (torch or hot plate)
4. Watch color spread
5. Quench at desired color

Color Indicates Temperature:
- Oxide layer thickness changes with temperature
- Thin film interference creates colors
- Reliable temperature indicator

SECTION 2.4: SPECIFIC PROJECTS

KNIFE FORGING:

Steel Selection:
- High carbon: 1075, 1084, 1095
- Tool steel: O1, A2
- Leaf spring (5160): Excellent knife steel
- Old files: Already hardened high carbon

Design:
1. Sketch pattern
2. Determine dimensions
3. Full tang vs stick tang
4. Blade geometry (bevel angle)

Forging Process:
1. Start with bar stock (1" × 1/4" × 6")
2. Draw out to length
3. Taper to point
4. Forge bevels (both sides)
5. Fuller to shape
6. Define tang
7. Punch guard holes if desired

Beveling:
- Angle anvil edge
- Strike blade at angle
- Creates bevel
- Work both sides evenly

Heat Treatment:
1. Normalize (heat orange, air cool) 3 times
2. Heat to non-magnetic (A3 temp)
3. Quench in oil (fast for water for simple steels)
4. Should be glass hard (file test)
5. Temper to straw-purple (350-400°F)

Grinding/Finishing:
1. Grind bevels (belt grinder)
2. Sand progressively (80, 120, 220, 400)
3. Polish if desired
4. Etch (ferric chloride) reveals pattern

Handle:
- Wood, bone, antler, synthetic
- Pin or epoxy
- Shape and sand
- Oil or finish

AXE MAKING:

Starting Material:
- Mild steel body
- High carbon bit (cutting edge)
- OR: Recycle old axe head

Process (Forge Welding Bit):
1. Mild steel bar, fold around mandrel (eye shape)
2. Forge weld folded end
3. Shape eye (proper size)
4. High carbon bit (V-shape)
5. Insert bit in mild steel body
6. Forge weld bit to body
7. Shape axe head
8. Drift eye to final size

Heat Treat:
1. Harden edge only (local heat)
2. Temper to purple-blue
3. Body stays soft (shock absorption)

Handle (Haft):
- Hickory (traditional)
- Ash, oak (acceptable)
- Shaped to fit eye
- Wedge secures

SWORD FORGING:

⚠️ Advanced Project:

Design:
- Length: 24-42 inches typical
- Width: 1.5-3 inches
- Thickness: 1/4" spine tapering to edge
- Fuller (groove) reduces weight

Steel:
- 5160 (leaf spring): Tough, flexible
- 1084: Good mid-range carbon
- Pattern welded (Damascus): Beautiful

Process:
1. Start large bar (1.5" × 3/8" × 36")
2. Draw out to length (48")
3. Forge bevels
4. Fuller down spine
5. Taper to point
6. Define tang
7. Normalize
8. Clay coating (differential hardening)
9. Heat treat (selective hardening)
10. Temper

Differential Hardening:
- Clay on spine (thick)
- Edge bare or thin clay
- Quench
- Edge hardens, spine stays soft
- Creates flexible, tough blade

Japanese Method (Hamon):
- Clay, ash, charcoal mix
- Applied to back/sides
- Edge left bare
- Produces visible line (hamon)
- Beautiful and functional

TOOLS (Hammer, Punch, Chisel):

Hammer Head:
1. Start with 2" square stock
2. Punch eye (drift to size)
3. Forge head shape
4. Harden face (quench face only)
5. Temper back of head (toughness)
6. Polish face

Punch:
1. Round stock (O1, A2 tool steel)
2. Taper to point
3. Harden tip (quench 1" of tip)
4. Temper to purple (280°C)
5. Tough, won't chip

Chisel:
1. Octagon or square stock
2. Forge cutting edge
3. Bevel edge
4. Harden edge (1-2 inches)
5. Temper dark purple (300-320°C)

DECORATIVE WORK:

Twists:
- Classic decoration
- Square or round stock
- Heat full section
- Twist with wrenches
- Count turns (symmetry)

Scrolls:
- Taper end
- Curl end on horn
- Progressive bending
- Multiple scrolls = complex patterns

Leaves:
1. Flatten section of bar
2. Cut leaf shape (hot or cold cut)
3. Texture veins (punch or chisel)
4. Curve/bend for 3D effect

Roses:
1. Forge thin disk
2. Cut spiral from outside in
3. Heat center
4. Twist spiral upward
5. Shape petals

Collars:
- Wrap around multiple pieces
- Drift tight
- Forge weld or rivet

SECTION 2.5: ADVANCED TECHNIQUES

PATTERN WELDING (Damascus):

Process:
1. Stack layers (alternate high/low carbon)
2. Wire tie bundle
3. Heat to welding temperature (1300°C)
4. Flux heavily (borax)
5. Forge weld (firm, overlapping blows)
6. Reheat and consolidate
7. Draw out to length
8. Cut, stack, repeat
9. Patterns emerge after multiple folds

Calculations:
- Start: 2 layers
- 1 fold: 4 layers
- 2 folds: 8 layers
- 5 folds: 64 layers
- 10 folds: 2,048 layers
- 15 folds: 65,536 layers

Patterns:
- Ladder: Grind perpendicular to length
- Raindrop: Punch small divots
- Twist: Twist bar, then flatten
- "W" pattern: Complex manipulation

Etching:
- Ferric chloride solution
- High carbon etches darker
- Reveals pattern
- 10-30 minutes soak

SAN MAI (Three Layer):

Construction:
- Low carbon sides
- High carbon core
- Forge weld together
- Grind reveals hard edge, soft sides

Benefits:
- Tough body
- Hard edge
- Easier than full pattern weld
- Less warping

MOKUME GANE (Wood Grain Metal):

Non-Ferrous Layers:
- Copper, brass, nickel silver
- Stack and clamp
- Heat in forge (800°C)
- Diffusion bond (no flux needed)
- Distort surface (punch, grind)
- Reveals pattern

Jewelry Applications:
- Rings, pendants
- Wood-like appearance
- Beautiful patterns

BRONZE/BRASS CASTING:

Lost Wax Method:
1. Carve wax model
2. Invest in plaster
3. Burn out wax (leaves cavity)
4. Pour molten bronze
5. Break plaster
6. Clean casting

Sand Casting:
1. Make pattern (wood)
2. Pack sand around pattern
3. Remove pattern
4. Pour molten metal in cavity
5. Break out casting
6. Clean, finish

Crucible:
- Clay-graphite crucible
- Heat in forge
- Lift with tongs
- Pour carefully

Pouring Temperature:
- Bronze: 1100-1150°C
- Brass: 950-1000°C
- Aluminum: 700-750°C

Safety:
- Face shield
- Leather gear
- Dry mold (steam explosion!)
- No water near molten metal
